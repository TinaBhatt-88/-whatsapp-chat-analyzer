Abstract:
Level 4 autonomous vehicles that reach the limits of their operational design domain may request remote assistance, requiring a fleet dispatcher to assign each request to a suitable human operator. Physiological workload estimates derived from electrocardiography (ECG) and electrodermal activity (EDA) could inform this decision, but such models are uncertain and often generalize poorly across individuals and datasets. We trained a subject-disjoint workload classifier on the public CL-Drive dataset and evaluated it, without adaptation, on the public CLARE dataset. We then replayed the model’s real held-out false positives, false negatives, calibration errors, and predictive entropy in a discrete-event fleet simulator implementing five allocation strategies. The proposed uncertainty-aware allocator reduced the influence of unreliable workload predictions and reverted to operational context when sensing was stale, degraded, missing, or highly uncertain. In a preregistered experiment using 500 paired simulation seeds per scenario mix, naive workload-aware allocation significantly increased the simulated unsafe-assignment rate in the visibility-heavy condition. Uncertainty gating prevented that statistically supported harm, but its improvement over a strong context-only baseline was neither statistically robust after Holm correction nor large enough to meet the preregistered practical-effect threshold. Three deterministic CARLA scenarios demonstrated the end-to-end pipeline but were not used as statistical evidence. The contribution is therefore a transparent uncertainty-gated architecture and prediction-error replay framework, not a claim of high workload-classification accuracy or real-world safety validation.
